# Plainspoken copy, no hype

Marketing and product copy says plainly what the product does and leaves out hyperbole, over-promises, fluffy metaphors and appeals to emotion.
Hyperbole is praise or scale beyond what the product facts support, such as "game-changing", "the best", "effortless" or "in seconds". An over-promise is a result or guarantee the product cannot ensure, such as "never lose a customer again" or "double your sales". A fluffy metaphor is a figure of speech standing in for what a feature does, such as "supercharge", "unlock", "on autopilot" or "magic". An appeal to emotion is a line that works on fear, envy, urgency or excitement instead of a fact, such as "don't get left behind" or "you deserve better". A literal, specific statement of what the product does, its limits, its price or how to start is fine.

Apply this to every piece of copy you write or change, for the whole task, and check it again
before you commit.

## Why

Our customers run small businesses and read marketing all day that is loud, vague and promises too
much. They come to us to get work done. Copy that says exactly what the product does, and what it
does not, is easier to trust and quicker to act on than copy that tries to excite them.

## How to write it

- Start from the product facts. Each sentence should be checkable against them: what the feature
  does, when, for whom, with what limit, at what price.
- Headlines, subject lines and first lines follow the rule too. That is where hype gathers. A good
  headline names the product and what it does.
- Selling is fine. When asked to sell harder, add facts: what the reader gets, what it costs, who
  it suits, how to start. Do not turn up the adjectives.
- State a result only if the facts state it. If there is no measured figure, there is no figure,
  and no "more sales", "higher open rates" or "customers love it" either.
- Keep the limits in. "Works when the customer has an account" is a fact the reader needs.
- A call to action is a plain verb: "Start a trial", "See the plans".

## Gotchas

Each of these breaks the rule without looking like a lie:

- Stock verbs: "supercharge", "unlock", "elevate", "transform", "boost", "take it to the next
  level".
- Praise words: "powerful", "seamless", "effortless", "smart", "stunning", "best-in-class".
- Promised outcomes: "win back every customer", "never miss a sale", "watch your revenue grow".
- Figures of speech: "your secret weapon", "while you sleep", "works like magic", "on autopilot".
- Pressure: "don't miss out", "stop leaving money on the table", "your competitors already do
  this", "you deserve better", invented deadlines.
- Hook questions and exclamations that work on worry or excitement: "Tired of ...?", "Big news!".

## Example

From a page about an invoicing tool's payment reminders.

Compliant:

```markdown
## Payment reminders

Ledgerly emails your customer three days before an invoice is due and again the day after. You
choose the wording. Reminders stop when the invoice is marked as paid.
```

Violating:

```markdown
## Get paid faster, stress-free

Say goodbye to chasing invoices! Ledgerly's smart reminders work around the clock so you never
wait for a payment again.
```

## Before you commit

Reread every piece of copy you created or changed, headlines and subject lines first. For each
claim ask: is this literally what the product does, and do the facts support it? Rewrite any line
that praises, promises a result, speaks in a figure of speech or leans on the reader's feelings,
then check again.
