# Pro is a paid plan with a refund, never a free trial

## The rule

Use "free trial" (or "try free") only when the customer pays nothing to try the product; a money-back guarantee or a paid first period with refund is described as a refund offer, not a free trial.

Inkwell Studio's only plan, Pro, costs £12 a month and is charged upfront when the customer subscribes, with a 30-day money-back guarantee; there is no free tier and no free trial. So Inkwell Studio copy never calls Pro, or trying it, free: no "free trial", "try free", "start free", "free for 30 days" or "risk-free trial", and nothing that says the customer starts without paying. It may say that Pro can be tried for 30 days and refunded in full. Creating an account costs nothing but gives watermarked previews only, not downloads; saying so is accurate, and it is not a trial of Pro.

Apply it to every page, ad and email you write or edit, for the whole task: body copy, headings,
headlines, buttons, link text, footnotes and the front matter (title, description, subject,
preheader).

## Why

A customer who clicks "Start your free trial" and is charged £12 at checkout has been misled, and
the UK advertising code says so in terms: "free trial" may not describe a "satisfaction or your
money back" offer. A refund the customer has to ask for is not the same as never being charged.
The honest offer is still a good one, so say it as it is.

## What counts

These break the rule for Pro:

- "free trial", "30-day free trial", "try free", "try Pro free", "start free", "start for free";
- "free for 30 days", "first month free", "30 days on us", "risk-free trial";
- anything that says the customer starts without paying: "no payment today", "no card required",
  "you won't be charged for 30 days", "try before you buy", "30 days, then £12 a month";
- any of the above with a correcting footnote. The footnote does not undo the headline.

These are fine:

- the price and when it is charged: "£12 a month, charged when you subscribe";
- the guarantee as a refund: "30-day money-back guarantee", "full refund within 30 days";
- inviting the reader to try Pro, with the refund beside it;
- "free" in another sense: "royalty-free", "creating an account is free", "feel free to reply".

Short formats are where this slips: a button or a 30-character ad headline has no room for a
caveat, so choose words that are true on their own, such as "Subscribe to Pro" or "30-Day
Money-Back Guarantee".

## Examples

Compliant:

> **Subscribe to Pro: £12 a month.** Not what you needed? Tell us within 30 days of your first
> payment and we will refund it in full.

Violating:

> **Start your 30-day free trial.** Cancel within 30 days and get your £12 back.

## Before you commit

Search each page, ad and email you changed for "free", "trial", "risk", "on us", "charge" and
"card". For each hit, ask whether a customer who read only that line would expect to pay £12
today. If not, rewrite it as the paid plan with its refund, then search again.
