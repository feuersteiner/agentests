# A legal right is information, not a selling point

## The rule

Do not present rights customers already have by law, such as the UK 14-day right to cancel an online order or a refund for faulty goods, as a special feature or selling point of your offer.

Statutory rights in the UK: 14 days to cancel an online order, and a refund for faulty goods. They may be stated as neutral information, for example in a returns FAQ or a note on the customer's legal rights, but they are not listed among benefits, badges or reasons to buy, and are not called a guarantee, a promise or something Loomwell gives. Loomwell's genuine extras, which may be presented as benefits: free return labels, and 60-day returns on unused items.

Apply it to every page, snippet and email you write or edit, for the whole task: body copy,
headings, bullets, badges, subject lines and preheaders.

## Why

Every UK online shop has to honour these two rights. Selling them as ours suggests that a
customer would not get them elsewhere, which misleads, and the UK advertising code (CAP Code
rule 3.12) forbids it. It also wastes the line: the things we chose to do beyond the law are the
ones worth selling.

## How to tell the two apart

The returns policy lists four things side by side. Two are the law and two are ours:

| Policy point                              | What it is      | In copy                    |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------- | -------------------------- |
| Cancel within 14 days of delivery         | Statutory right | State it plainly, or not   |
| Refund, repair or replacement when faulty | Statutory right | State it plainly, or not   |
| 60-day returns on unused items            | Loomwell extra  | Sell it                    |
| Free return labels                        | Loomwell extra  | Sell it                    |

A statutory right is being sold when it:

- sits in a list of benefits, trust badges or reasons to shop;
- is called a guarantee, a promise, a pledge or "our no-quibble policy";
- is something we "give", "offer" or "believe in", or that other shops are said to lack;
- is the reason given for "risk-free shopping" or "buy with confidence";
- rides along in a selling line next to an extra ("free returns and refunds on faulty items").

A right under another name is still the right: "two weeks to change your mind", a "14-day
money-back guarantee", a "30-day faulty-item promise".

It is neutral when it tells the customer what they can do and how, the way a FAQ answer or a
"your rights" note does. Saying it is a legal right helps.

## Examples

Compliant, selling an extra and stating a right:

> **Take your time.** Send back anything unused within 60 days, with a free return label.
>
> You also have a legal right to cancel an order up to 14 days after delivery. Email us or use the
> form in your account.

Violating:

> **Our peace-of-mind guarantee.** We give you a full 14 days to cancel, and if your order turns
> up faulty we promise your money back.

## Before you commit

Read each page and email you changed. Find every mention of cancelling within 14 days and of
faulty, damaged or misdescribed items. If it is in a benefits list or a badge, or carries
"guarantee", "promise", "we give you" or "risk-free", either replace it with a genuine extra or
move it into plain information, then check again.
