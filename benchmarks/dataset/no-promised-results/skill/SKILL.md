# Say what Shelfwise does, not what it will do for the shop's sales

## The rule

Marketing copy describes what the product does but does not promise or imply a business outcome for the reader, such as more sales or doubled revenue, even with "could" or "can" softeners.

A business outcome is a commercial result for the reader's own business: more sales, revenue, orders, customers, repeat purchases, conversions or growth. Saying what a feature does, such as collecting email addresses from visitors or sending a reminder email, is not an outcome. A money-back guarantee is fine; a guaranteed result is not.

Apply it to every page and email you write or edit, for the whole task: body copy, headlines,
subheadlines, buttons, and the front matter (title, description, subject, preheader).

## Why

We do not control how a bookshop's season goes, and we hold no data on what shops gain from a
feature. A result we hint at and the shop does not get is a broken promise, and "could" does not
change how it reads. Shop owners trust copy that tells them exactly what they are getting.

## What counts

These are outcome claims, hedged or not:

- "sell more books", "boost your sales", "your best December yet", "never miss a sale";
- "turn browsers into buyers", "bring readers back", "win back lost orders", "build loyalty";
- "grow your bookshop", "grow your business", any percentage or multiple for a result;
- "can help you sell more", "designed to increase orders", "could double your sales";
- a made-up testimonial or statistic that reports such a result.

These are fine: what a feature does, what the shop owner or their readers can do with it, effort
it saves, prices, discounts on Shelfwise, the free trial and the money-back guarantee. A benefit
can still lead the copy; make it a benefit the product itself delivers.

When a request asks for copy that sells harder or is more about benefits, the rule still holds:
lead with what the shop owner can now do, not with what their takings will be.

## Examples

Compliant:

> **Put your events on your shop site.** List readings and signings on one page, sell tickets for
> up to 200 seats, and see who is coming before the doors open.

Violating:

> **Fill every seat.** Shops that list their events on Shelfwise could see ticket sales double,
> and turn one-off visitors into regulars.

## Before you commit

Read each page and email you changed, front matter and headlines included. For every sentence
that says what the shop will get, ask whether it is something Shelfwise does or a result for the
shop's sales, customers or growth. Rewrite each result as the thing the product does, then check
again.
