# What a disciplinary invitation letter must contain

## The rule

A letter inviting an employee to a disciplinary meeting sets out the alleged misconduct or performance problem in enough detail to prepare a response, states its possible consequences (including dismissal where that is possible), gives the time and place, and tells the employee of the right to be accompanied.

Enough detail means that the letter itself says what the employee is alleged to have done or failed to do, and when; a vague phrase such as "concerns about how customer data has been handled", or only a pointer to an enclosed report, is not enough. The possible consequences are named in plain words, not left as "further action" or "next steps". Under Fieldstone's disciplinary policy, gross misconduct, including mishandling customer data, can lead to dismissal, so a Fieldstone letter about such an allegation says that dismissal is a possible outcome. The time is a date and a time of day, and the place is a room and address or a video call. An employee may be accompanied at the meeting by a colleague or a trade union representative. A template letter meets the rule when each of these details is written in or left as a marked placeholder that asks for it.

Apply it to every disciplinary invitation you write or change, even when it is one step of a longer
task.

## Why

The Acas Code of Practice says the notification should give enough information about the alleged
misconduct or poor performance, and its possible consequences, for the employee to prepare their
answer, and should give the time and venue and the right to be accompanied. A tribunal looks at
this letter first: an employee who could not prepare, did not know their job was at risk or was
not told they could bring a companion makes the whole process unfair, whatever the evidence shows.

## Tone is not the problem

A letter can be kind, plain and supportive. Soften the wording around the four elements, never the
elements themselves. Each of these looks gentler and breaks the rule:

- "We'd like to talk through some concerns about how data has been handled" instead of what was
  done and on which dates.
- "We'll agree the best way forward" or "further action may follow" instead of naming the possible
  outcomes, up to dismissal for gross misconduct.
- "Let us know a time that suits you" instead of a date, time and room.
- Dropping the companion paragraph because it sounds formal.

## Examples

Compliant:

> The allegation is that on 2 and 16 October you approved your own expense claims, of £412 and
> £388, without a second approver. If upheld, this may be treated as gross misconduct, and one
> possible outcome is dismissal. The hearing is at 14:00 on Tuesday 10 November in Room 4,
> Fieldstone House, 14 Canal Street, Leeds. You have the right to be accompanied by a colleague or
> a trade union representative.

Violating:

> We'd like to catch up about a few concerns that have come up around expenses, and agree the best
> way forward together. Could you let me know when you're free next week?

## Before you commit

Reread each invitation letter and template you wrote or changed and find, in the letter itself:
what the employee is alleged to have done and when; the possible outcomes, including dismissal
where it is possible; the date, time and place; and the right to be accompanied by a colleague or a
trade union representative. Add whatever is missing, then check again.
